# Improved prediction and risk stratification of major adverse cardiovascular events using an explainable machine learning approach combining plasma biomarkers and traditional risk factors 

Xi-Ru Zhang<sup>1,2</sup> , Wen-Fang Zhong<sup>2</sup> , Rui-Yan Liu<sup>2</sup> , Jie-Lin Huang<sup>1</sup> , Jing-Xiang Fu<sup>1</sup> , Jian Gao<sup>1,2</sup> , Pei-Dong Zhang<sup>2</sup> , Dan Liu<sup>2</sup> , Zhi-Hao Li<sup>2</sup> , Yan He<sup>1,3</sup> , Hongwei Zhou<sup>1,3*</sup> and Zhuang Li<sup>1,3*</sup> 

### **Abstract** 

**Background** Cardiovascular diseases (CVD) remain the leading cause of morbidity and mortality globally. Traditional risk models, primarily based on established risk factors, often lack the precision needed to accurately predict newonset major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE). This study aimed to improve prediction and risk stratification by integrating traditional risk factors with biochemical and metabolomic biomarkers. 

**Methods** We analyzed data from 229,352 participants in the UK Biobank (median age 58.0 years; 45.4% male) who were free of baseline MACE. Biomarker selection was conducted using area under the curve (AUC), minimal joint mutual information maximization (JMIM), and correlation analyses, while Cox proportional hazards models were employed to evaluate the predictive performance of combined traditional risk factors and biomarkers. Optimal binary thresholds were determined utilizing CatBoost and SHAP, leading to the calculation of a Biomarker Risk Score (BRS) for each participant. Multivariable Cox models were conducted to assess the associations of each concerned biomarker and BRS with new-onset endpoints. 

**Results** The combination of PANEL + All Biochemistry + Cor0.95 of Nonov Met predictors demonstrated significantly improved discriminative performance compared to traditional models, such as Age + Sex and ASCVD, across all endpoints. Although the prediction for hemorrhagic stroke was suboptimal (C-index = 0.699), C-index values for other outcomes surpassed 0.75, with the highest value (0.822) recorded for CVD-related mortality. Key predictors of newonset MACE included cystatin C, HbA1c, GlycA, and GGT, while IGF-1 and DHA exhibited potential protective effects. The BRS stratified individuals into low-, intermediate-, and high-risk groups, with the strongest effect observed for CVD death, where the high-risk group had a relative risk of 2.76 (95% CI 2.48–3.07) compared to the low-risk group.

### **Introduction** 

Cardiovascular diseases (CVD) remain the leading cause of mortality and disability worldwide, posing a significant burden on public health systems and societies at large 

[1, 2]. Effective prevention and management of CVD are contingent upon accurate prediction and risk stratification [3]. Traditional risk prediction models, such as the ESC SCORE2 and AHA-ASCVD, have provided essential 

Page 3 of 19 

Zhang _et al. Cardiovascular Diabetology_ (2025) 24:153 

tools for assessing CVD risk based on established clinical and demographic factors, including age, sex, blood pressure, cholesterol levels, smoking status, and diabetes [4, 5]. However, despite their widespread adoption in clinical practice, these models often neglect emerging biomarkers that play pivotal roles in CVD pathogenesis. Key among these biomarkers are glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), glycoprotein acetyls (GlycA), cystatin C and omega-3, which have been independently associated with CVD outcomes and related mortality [6–13]. The failure to routinely incorporate these biomarkers into risk prediction models underscores a critical gap in our understanding of CVD risk and its modifiable components. 

Recent advances in high-throughput technologies, particularly nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy, have transformed the field of metabolomics, enabling the comprehensive and standardized quantification of a diverse array of small molecules circulating in the blood [3, 12]. This capability allows for an in-depth exploration of the complex metabolic pathways underlying disease processes. The ability to profile numerous metabolites simultaneously presents a unique opportunity to identify novel biomarkers and enhance our understanding of their roles in disease etiology. In the context of CVD, these technological advancements have broadened our perspective beyond traditional risk factors, uncovering a more extensive spectrum of metabolic signatures associated with cardiovascular health [3, 12, 14, 15]. Nevertheless, the systematic integration of clinical, metabolic, and traditional risk factors to enhance CVD prediction remains an underexplored domain. Furthermore, the interpretability and clinical relevance of these biomarkers in relation to specific CVD outcomes, such as major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), have yet to be fully elucidated. 

This study seeks to address this gap by integrating clinical biochemistry markers, NMR-based metabolomics data, and traditional CVD risk factors to enhance the prediction of incident MACE. Utilizing data from the UK Biobank—a large, population-based cohort—we assess the predictive performance of these combined biomarkers through Cox proportional hazards models and machine learning techniques, specifically SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), to identify the most significant predictors and their optimal thresholds [16–18]. Our approach not only advances our understanding of the individual and collective roles of these biomarkers but also facilitates the development of a biomarker risk index (BRI) and biomarker risk score (BRS), which could significantly refine risk stratification in clinical practice. Ultimately, this study offers novel insights into the integration of innovative biomarkers into CVD risk prediction models, paving the way for the early identification and intervention of high-risk individuals. 

### **Methods** 

#### **Study designs and populations** 

The UK Biobank is an ongoing population-based prospective cohort study that features extensive phenotypic data and biomarkers measure. Between 2006 and 2010, more than 500,000 participants aged 40–69 years from the general population were recruited at 22 assessment centers across the United Kingdom. Sociodemographic information, lifestyle and environmental factors, medications information and family history were collected through touchscreen questionnaire or verbal interview at recruitment. Pre-existing health and medical conditions were obtained from the baseline questionnaire and interview, as well as hospital records and death registries. Anthropometric measurements and blood sample collection and laboratory measures were taken at recruitment. The protocol for handling and storing blood samples has been detailed in prior publications [3, 6, 18, 19]. For the purpose of our analyses, we excluded individuals with any type of MACE or entirely missing biochemistry marker data at baseline from those randomly selected for NMR-based metabolomic profiling, resulting in an analytic cohort of 229,352 participants. 

#### **Conventional predictor and biomarker set** 

The overview of the study workflow, as well as clinical predictor sets and biomarker sets were presented in Fig. 1. We investigated three sets of conventional clinical predictor variables: Age + Sex, ASCVD and PANEL. Cardiovascular predictors set (ASCVD) were selected according to the cardiovascular risk scores for primary prevention recommended by the ESC and AHA, including the ESC SCORE2 and the AHA-ASCVD score [4, 5]. The PANEL predictor set included a comprehensive range of demographics, lifestyle, physical measurements, medical history, medications and the CVD family history. A list of all clinical predictors applied in this study is presented in Supplementary Table 1 and a list of all metabolomic predictors in Supplementary Table 2. A total of 30 conventional clinical biochemistry markers were measured from blood samples collected at recruitment. After excluding variables with over 20% missing values, substantial sex differences, or substantial variations based on fasting duration, 26 biochemical markers were included in the analysis. Furthermore, a random subset of 274,236 participants was characterized using a high-throughput NMR metabolomics platform developed by Nightingale Health Ltd. The NMR assay covers 170 original biomarkers spanning multiple metabolic pathways including amino acids, fatty acids, glycolysis metabolites, ketone bodies, inflammation markers as well as cholesterol subtypes and lipoprotein lipids in 14 subclasses [3, 12]. The new-onset MACE was defined as a composite of the following events: incident myocardial infarction, incident stroke classified as either ischemic or hemorrhagic, death

from CVD, and the occurrence of unstable angina, representing a range of serious cardiovascular outcomes that may significantly impact patient prognosis [9, 20]. We investigated the composite endpoint defined as MACE, along with each of its components, derived from hospital episode statistics and national death registries information. Incident and mortality outcomes were determined based on the first documented occurrence of relevant events, identified using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision (ICD-10) or ICD-9 codes [13, 21]. #### **Statistical analysis** 

#### **_Data imputation and baseline characteristics summary_** 

For the potential conventional risk factor, we assumed that the missing data occurred at random and conducted multiple imputations using chained equations with random forests [22, 23]. This approach generated five datasets, from which one was randomly selected for subsequent analysis. Missing values in any covariate were less than 20%. To minimize the impact of outliers for the original metabolites, plasma biomarker values exceeding four interquartile ranges from the median were replaced with their corresponding boundary values [12]. Missing values for any metabolite were below 5% and were imputed using a random forest algorithm. The baseline characteristics of the overall population and specific datasets (incident MACE, stroke, death from CVD, myocardial infarction, unstable angina) were presented as median (interquartile range [IQR]) for continuous variables, and number (percentage [%]) for categorical variables. 

#### **_Biomarkers selection and model predictive performance_** 

To preliminarily identify biomarkers for enhancing prediction and risk stratification of new-onset MACE, we considered several sets of biomarkers, each incorporating different combinations of clinical biochemical and metabolic markers. These sets included: (1) all selected conventional clinical biochemical markers (All Bioche), (2) all measured original NMR metabolic markers (All Met), (3) all selected biochemical markers combined with the top 50 non-overlapping metabolic markers selected based on the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) (All Bioche + AUCTop50 of Nonov Met), (4) all selected biochemical markers combined with the top 50 non-overlapping metabolic markers selected based on minimal joint mutual information 

maximization (JMIM) values (All Bioche + JMIMTop50 of Nonov Met), and (5) all selected biochemical markers combined with non-overlapping metabolic markers having Spearman correlation coefficients less than 0.95 (All Bioche + Cor0.95 of Nonov Met) [24]. The naming of the biomarker sets and their contents is further illustrated in Fig. 1C. 

These sets were incorporated into Cox proportional hazards model, and Harrell’s C-index along with 95% confidence interval (CI) was employed to assess model’s discrimination performance. The 95% CI was calculated using the bootstrap method, with resampling performed 1000 times [25]. Non-overlapping metabolic markers were selected using the Filter method from the _mlr3_ package, which serves as a preprocessing step prior to model training. For the AUCTop50 of Nonov Met set, we applied the flt(“auc”) method to separately calculate the AUC for each non-overlapping metabolite in predicting the target variable. The top 50 metabolites with the highest AUC values were then selected. Similarly, for the JMIMTop50 of Nonov Met, we used the flt(“jmim”) method to separately compute JMIM score for each feature, which indicates the degree of information shared between independent variable and the target variable. The top 50 features with the highest JMIM score were selected. In the Cor0.95 of Nonov Met subset, we performed a correlation-based variable selection approach to identify and eliminate highly correlated predictors. We computed the Spearman correlation matrix among all non-overlapping metabolites and applied a threshold of 0.95 to identify variable pairs with high correlation. Variables with correlation coefficients of 0.95 or higher were considered redundant. To improve computational efficiency, we used an exact method for correlation matrices with fewer than 100 variables and an approximate method for larger matrices. This strategy effectively reduced multicollinearity, ensuring the robustness of our subsequent analyses. Ultimately, 48 metabolites with Spearman correlation coefficients less than 0.95 were retained. Taking into account both performance and complexity of established model, we selected combined “All Bioche + Cor0.95 of Nonov Met” set as biomarkers for the risk stratification of new-onset MACE and each of its individual components. Subsequently, we modeled disease risk for each endpoint using Cox proportional hazards models based on the previously defined conventional clinical predictor sets: Age + Sex, ASCVD, and PANEL. For all sets, the performance of the Cox proportional hazards models was benchmarked against those based on the combinations of these conventional sets with the selected biomarkers sets, which including 26 biochemistry and 48 metabolic markers, making us to assess whether the selected biomarkers could improve the predictive performance of the models [3].

#### **_Feature importance and BRI extraction_** 

The selected biomarkers, along with sex, age, and body mass index (BMI), were incorporated as features to develop CatBoost classifiers for predicting incident MACE and each of its components. While the relationship between BMI and vascular health can be complex and potentially non-linear, given the well-established association between BMI and cardiovascular outcomes [21, 26–30], as well as its role as a key indicator of body composition, and its correlation with various metabolic factors [31, 32], we believe BMI should be considered a critical variable in cardiovascular risk prediction. CatBoost is an unbiased boosting algorithm with support for categorical features. It processes data using ordered boosting and permutation-based statistics, which helps minimize prediction shift, reduces overfitting, and improves model generalization. Additionally, CatBoost’s optimized GPU implementation enables faster training on large datasets, making it scalable for large-scale tasks. Empirical results have demonstrated that CatBoost consistently outperforms other gradient boosting models, such as XGBoost and LightGBM, both in terms of accuracy and efficiency. These advantages, combined with its ability to provide interpretable results through SHAP values, make CatBoost a reliable and efficient choice for practical machine learning applications, especially in fields requiring high transparency and accuracy [33–35]. 

For each endpoint, the data was randomly split into training (70%) and validation (30%) sets. Hyperparameter tuning and model fitting were conducted on the training set, while the validation set was used to evaluate model performance and determine optimal hyperparameters. The final model was then fitted on the entire dataset using the identified the optimal hyperparameters, and the SHAP value [17, 33, 36] were calculated to interpret the output of CatBoost models [33–35]. SHAP values fairly distribute the “contribution” of each feature to the model's prediction, quantifying how much each feature increases or decreases a specific prediction compared to the average across the dataset. Feature importance was visualized using the SHAP summary plot, which aggregates SHAP values to identify the most influential predictors in the model. The BRI is derived from the probabilities predicted by the CatBoost classifier for each endpoint. Specifically, the CatBoost classifier outputs the risk probabilities for the target variable, where each individual’s risk is expressed as a probability ranging between 0 and 1. These probabilities represent the likelihood that an individual will experience the endpoint of interest. For each endpoint, we categorized individuals into five risk groups based on the quintiles of the BRI and plotted the cumulative risk for individuals in the first, third, and fifth quintile groups. 

Notably, the CatBoost model we employed utilizes gradient boosting decision trees, which inherently capture complex relationships between variables through the splitting mechanism. This is particularly useful for handing high-dimensional or nonlinear data. As a result, even with a nonlinear relationship between continuous variables and the target variable, CatBoost can automatically learn these patterns without the need for additional feature engineering [17]. To explore potential non-linear relationships between BMI and new-onset MACE, as well as its components, we conducted sensitivity analyses by fitting restricted cubic splines to a multivariableadjusted Cox proportional hazards model (RCS-Cox). We evaluated the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) for models with 3–7 knots and selected the model with 3 knots (at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles) based on the principle of minimizing AIC. 

#### **_SHAP-based feature interpretation, BRS and risk assessment_** 

We first identified several meaningful variables that were consistently ranked in the top 50 of importance for predicting different types of MACE outcomes using CatBoost classifiers and SHAP analysis. The multivariable Cox proportional hazards model, adjusted for age, sex, and BMI, was then applied to estimate the risk of newonset MACE and its components for each one standard deviation (SD) change in these variables. Next, SHAP dependence plots were generated to visualize the importance of features, along with their approximate optimal binary thresholds and the direction associated with MACE and each of its components. The X-axis represents the original values of each given feature, while the Y-axis shows the corresponding SHAP value. A SHAP value greater than zero indicates that the feature contributes to a higher probability prediction (e.g., predicting ‘1’); while the SHAP value less than zero drives the prediction toward a lower probability (e.g., predicting'0'). That is to say a SHAP value above zero suggests that the feature may increase the risk of new-onset MACE. Based on whether the SHAP value is greater than or equal to zero, we categorize the feature as a binary variable. Each feature was then binarized based on its estimated optimal threshold. We further applied the Cox proportional hazards model, adjusted for sex, age, and BMI, to evaluate the association between the top 20 features (categorized as greater than or equal to, or less than, their respective optimal thresholds) and the risk of incident MACE and its components, compared to values below the threshold.

### **Results** 

#### **Study population characteristics** 

The study included 229,352 participants who were free of MACE at baseline, with a median age of 58.0 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 50.0–63.0). Of these participants, 45.4% were male, and 95.1% were of White ancestry. Over a median follow-up period of 13.66 years (IQR: 12.79–14.35), 16,333 participants experienced a confirmed first MACE. Individuals who developed MACE were more likely to have lower educational attainment and income levels, as well as higher plasma concentrations of c-reactive protein (CRP), HbA1c, and triglycerides (TG), in addition to higher waits circumference, hip circumference, body mass index (BMI), systolic blood pressure (SBP), and diastolic blood pressure (DBP). Furthermore, individuals who developed MACE were more likely to have prevalent hypertension or type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) at baseline and to report a family history of cardiovascular disease (Table 1). 

#### **Superior discriminative performance of combined biomarkers in cardiovascular risk stratification** 

Various methods were employed to select biomarkers, with predictive performance evaluated using Harrell's C-index and corresponding 95% CI. When the Cox proportional hazards model was constructed using the feature subset of All Bioche + Cor0.95 of Nonov Met, the model achieved a C-index of 0.698 (95% CI 0.695– 0.703), slightly outperforming models that included All Bioche + AUCTop50 of Nonov Met (C-index = 0.692, 95% CI 0.689–0.697) and All Bioche + JMIMTop50 of Nonov Met (C-index = 0.692, 95% CI 0.690–0.698). For models incorporating the PANEL variables, the combination of PANEL + All Bioche + Nonov Met and PANEL + All Bioche + Cor0.95 of Nonov Met yielded C-indices of 0.753 (95% CI 0.751–0.758) and 0.752 (95% CI 0.749– 0.756), respectively. Given its superior performance, reduced complexity, and lower multicollinearity, All Bioche + Cor0.95 of Nonov Met was selected as the biomarker subset for subsequent analyses (Supplementary Fig. 2). 

Incorporating the selected biomarkers into prediction models resulted in a substantial improvement in discriminative performance for all endpoints. The full model, combining PANEL predictors with the selected biomarkers, demonstrated enhanced predictive capability across all cardiovascular outcomes, outperforming the base models. While the predictive performance for hemorrhagic stroke was suboptimal (C-index = 0.699; 95% CI 0.697–0.719), C-indices for other endpoints exceeded 0.75, with the highest performance observed for predicting CVD-related death (C-index = 0.822, 95% CI 0.816–0.826). These results underscore the value of incorporating biomarkers to improve model accuracy 

and refine risk stratification for cardiovascular outcomes (Fig. 2). 

#### **CatBoost classifier and SHAP analysis reveals key factors influencing the prediction of MACE** 

To assess the impact of selected biomarkers, along with age, sex, and BMI, on CatBoost model predictions, we employed SHAP importance plots. Age was identified as the most influential factor in predicting MACE. Older participants (represented in dark purple) were more likely to develop MACE (right side), whereas younger participants (depicted in orange-yellow) had a lower likelihood of experiencing MACE and were more likely to remain healthy (left side). Cystatin C ranked third in importance, with higher levels correlating with an increased risk of incident MACE. In contrast, albumin and insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1) ranked eighth and ninth, with higher levels linked to a reduced risk, while lower levels were associated with an increased risk (Fig. 3A). 

SHAP dependence plots were utilized to visualize how changes in individual features affected model predictions and to explore the interactions between features. For MACE prediction, individuals aged 60 years or older had higher SHAP values, indicating a greater risk of newonset MACE compared to those younger than 60 years (Fig. 3B). Similarly, cystatin C levels ≥ 1.0 mg/L (Fig. 3D) or HbA1c concentrations ≥ 39.0 mmol/mol (Fig. 3E) were associated with an increased risk of MACE. ### **Discussion**

contribution and importance ranking, as well as estimating the optimal binary thresholds for each variable and illustrating their relationship with predicted probabilities (e.g., age ≥ 60 years, cystatin C ≥ 1.0 mg/L). This innovative approach addresses the limitations of “black-box” models, holding promise for enhancing cardiovascular risk prediction. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to report the importance ranking and corresponding binary cutoff values of chemical and metabolic biomarkers for predicting incident MACE and its components. Additionally, multiple variable selection strategies, including AUC, JMIM, and correlation analysis, were employed. In determining the optimal predictor subset, we accounted for not only the predictive performance of the model (C-index) but also the number of variables and the model's complexity. Our parsimonious models yielded a C-index of 0.752 (95% CI 0.749–0.756) for MACE incidence, demonstrating comparable performance to the full model, which combined all measured overlapping metabolic biomarkers, the selected 26 clinical chemistry biomarkers, and the defined PANEL information (C-index: 0.753; 95% CI 0.751–0.758). Overall, the final predictor subset, “PANEL + All Bioche + Cor0.95 of Nonov Met,” achieved a satisfactory balance between predictive accuracy and model simplicity. 

The study explored the improvement in discriminatory performance achieved by biomarkers through comparisons with traditional predictive sets, such as Age + Sex, ASCVD, and PANEL. While conventional cardiovascular risk assessment tools predominantly rely on demographic characteristics and lipid-related indicators, our study incorporated a broader range of biomarkers from diverse metabolic pathways, such as cystatin C, GGT, GlycA, and DHA, allowing for the evaluation of additional modifiable risk factors. Notably, we observed heterogeneity in the optimal binary thresholds of key variables across different disease endpoints. For instance, the binary thresholds for age, GlycA, IGF-1, and omega-3 varied between risk stratifications for MACE and its components. These findings provide a valuable foundation for developing personalized interventions tailored to specific cardiovascular outcomes. 

By integrating an extensive range of biomarkers with traditional CVD risk factors, this study proposes a more comprehensive framework for CVD risk assessment, facilitating the optimization of personalized prevention strategies. For example, individuals with cystatin C, GlycA, or HbA1c levels exceeding specific thresholds could be prioritized for targeted interventions. 

Furthermore, these findings support public health policymakers in stratifying moderate- or high-risk populations, thereby improving the allocation of healthcare resources. If the biomarkers used to generate BRI and BRS are routinely measured in clinical practice in the near future, they may offer a promising tool for the precise identification and early intervention of high-risk populations. Unfortunately, some of these biomarkers are not currently part of routine clinical testing. The widespread application of this multi-biomarker-based cardiovascular prediction and risk stratification strategy in clinical practice will depend on the future accessibility, validation, and standardization of these biomarkers. 

This study underscores the critical role of specific biomarkers in predicting and stratifying risk for MACE through data analysis and machine learning algorithms. These biomarkers are biologically relevant, playing significant roles regulating physiological functions associated with CVD. Rather than relying exclusively on statistical significance, we explore several potential biological mechanisms that may explain the associations between biomarkers and CVD outcomes. These mechanisms are rooted in well-established biological pathways, which lend biological plausibility to the observed associations and enhance their clinical relevance. Cystatin C is linked to elevated monocyte counts that contribute to plaque formation in arterial walls; high levels of Cystatin C often indicate renal dysfunction, which is associated with increased carotid intima-media thickness and heightened atherosclerosis risk [7, 8, 44, 45]. GlycA acts as a vital marker of chronic inflammation, reflecting the overall systemic inflammatory burden and playing a key role in the pathogenesis of various diseases, including cardiovascular events. As a composite biomarker, GlycA is particularly sensitive to fluctuations in the inflammatory response, capturing subtle alterations in systemic inflammation that may not be detected by traditional markers such as CRP. This unique sensitivity allows GlycA to identify individuals at elevated cardiovascular risk more effectively, especially when assessed alongside other established risk factors [10, 46]. HbA1c, a marker of long-term glucose control, is strongly correlated with the onset and progression of CVD, as chronic hyperglycemia leads to oxidative stress, resulting in endothelial dysfunction and atherosclerosis through advanced glycation end-products (AGEs) [11, 47, 48]. IGF-1 plays

Several previous studies suggested that elevated GGT levels contribute to prooxidant activity, leading to oxidative damage at the cellular and molecular levels, including tissue and DNA damage, which can play a crucial role in cardiovascular disease and other chronic conditions [52–54]. Additionally, DHA, an omega-3 fatty acid, influences various risk factors, including triglyceride levels and inflammation, thereby highlighting its protective effects against CVD [19, 55, 56]. These insights further validate that our machine learning model has accurately identified these key biomarkers related to MACE, demonstrating their scientific significance in prediction and risk stratification. In conclusion, this study demonstrates the potential of integrating traditional risk factors with biochemical and metabolomic biomarkers to enhance prediction and risk stratification for new-onset MACE and its components. By employing advanced machine learning models and SHAP analysis, we identified key biomarkers with significant predictive power, including Cystatin C, HbA1c, GlycA, and GGT, which can facilitate the early identification of high-risk individuals. These findings suggest that a multi-biomarker approach could refine CVD risk evaluation and guide targeted prevention strategies, with possible implications for clinical practice and public health, pending further validation and standardization. 

#### **Limitations** 

Although this study benefits from several strengths, including a large-scale population sample, an extended follow-up duration, comprehensive clinical biochemical measurements, and high-throughput metabolomics data that enabled the replication of previous findings and the discovery of novel blood biomarkers, there are notable limitations that should be considered in the interpretation of the results. 

Firstly, the prediction models were developed using a population aged 40 to 69 years, limiting their generalizability to older individuals, who have a notably higher risk of incident CVD. Future research should include a broader age range, particularly older populations, to enhance the generalizability of these models and improve their applicability in high-risk groups. Additionally, the study sample predominantly comprised individuals of European descent from the UK Biobank, and the demographic, lifestyle, and health-related characteristics of UK Biobank participants may differ from those of other populations, restricting the external validity of our findings. This makes it difficult to directly generalize our results to populations with different racial, health behavior, or socio-economic characteristics. Future studies should include more diverse populations to validate and 

extend our findings. Another limitation of this study is the absence of an external validation cohort. Without validation in an independent population, the generalizability and robustness of the proposed models remain uncertain. While our findings are promising, the applicability of the models to other populations needs to be further confirmed. Future studies should focus on validating our results in diverse, independent cohorts to assess the external validity of our model and its performance across different demographic and clinical groups. Fourth, while this innovative approach addresses the limitations of'black-box' models and holds promise for enhancing cardiovascular risk prediction, we recognize that several biomarkers identified and further used in the BRI and BRS proposed in this study are not routinely measured in clinical laboratories, which limits the immediate applicability and ease of use of these scores in clinical settings at present. Fifth, the study did not provide an estimation of absolute risk for individual biomarkers, which limits the clinical applicability of the findings. Including absolute risk calculations would enhance the practical utility of the results, as relative risk measures alone may not fully convey the real-world impact of the identified associations. Furthermore, while the UK Biobank offers data on 170 circulating metabolites, it does not provide a complete representation of the metabolome, with a predominant focus on metabolites related to lipoproteins and lipids. This limitation constrains our ability to investigate the potential contributions of other metabolite classes, such as amino acids and fatty acids, to CVD risk prediction. Notably, the estimation of binary thresholds for biomarkers used to predict various types of MACE onset may be considered relatively coarse. Nevertheless, the thresholds established for HbA1c and HDL-C are largely consistent with current clinical guidelines, partially reinforcing the reliability of our binary classification method. Finally, although we have proposed approximate binary thresholds for various biomarkers, the implementation of these metabolite thresholds in real-world settings must take into account factors such as variations in testing methodologies, which could influence metabolite concentration measurements. In summary, while this study presents important findings, future research should focus on addressing these limitations to enhance the applicability and generalizability of the results.
